Create a square hero-style conceptual illustration that complements the text provided above.

The illustration should not attempt to represent every point, detail, step, or nuance from the text. Instead, thoughtfully identify the one most visually meaningful idea, tension, relationship, or mechanism in the text and create a clean illustrative image around that core concept. The image should support and clarify the text, not replace it, summarize it, or over-explain it.

Primary goal:
Design the image as a complementary visual companion to the written content. It should feel like a polished hero illustration for an article, essay, presentation, or product narrative. The visual should help the viewer intuitively grasp the central idea, especially the parts that are best conveyed visually, while leaving the written text to carry the full explanation.

Interpretation instructions:
- Read the provided text and identify the most important concept that benefits from visual explanation.
- Prioritize the central metaphor, relationship, transformation, contrast, system, flow, or insight.
- Do not illustrate every sentence or every supporting detail.
- Avoid making the image feel like a literal checklist, dense infographic, or exhaustive diagram.
- The image should be selective, editorial, and interpretive.
- When choosing what to visualize, favor clarity, metaphor, and conceptual usefulness over completeness.
- The final result should feel like it belongs beside the text as a visual anchor.

Visual style:
- Use a clean, professional line-art style.
- The illustration should feel modern, precise, calm, and intelligent.
- Use crisp, vector-like lines with consistent stroke weight.
- Prefer thin to medium lines, rounded line caps, and simple geometric forms.
- The style should be polished and editorial, not sketchy, cartoonish, childish, messy, or decorative.
- Avoid photorealism, 3D rendering, heavy shading, painterly effects, or stock-illustration character scenes.

Background:
- Square aspect ratio, 1:1.
- Background must be solid pure white: #FFFFFF.
- No texture, gradients, shadows, borders, paper grain, vignette, or background pattern.
- Leave negative space, but do not let the image feel sparse, tiny, or under-composed.

Scale and space usage:
- The main illustration should make strong use of the square canvas.
- The visual subject should occupy a substantial portion of the available area, roughly 70–85% of the canvas by height or width, depending on the composition.
- Avoid placing a small diagram in the exact center with large empty bands above, below, or around it.
- Keep comfortable margins, but do not leave excessive blank space.
- The composition should feel full, intentional, and well-scaled within the square.
- Allow the illustration to extend vertically and horizontally as appropriate, while still preserving a clean premium feel.
- Use supporting elements, spacing, and layout to balance the full canvas rather than clustering everything in the middle.
- The image should feel ready to display large on a screen without appearing undersized.
- Do not crop important elements at the edges, but the composition may come close enough to the margins to feel visually substantial.

Use of color:
- Mostly monochrome line art with small, purposeful splashes of color.
- Use color only to highlight the most important conceptual elements.
- Limit accent colors to 2 to 4 total.
- Accent colors should be refined, modern, and slightly muted.
- Avoid neon, overly saturated, rainbow palettes, or large filled color areas.
- Color should guide attention, not dominate the image.

Composition:
- Make the composition feel like a hero image, not a technical diagram.
- Center the main visual idea clearly, but scale it large enough to visually command the canvas.
- Use a small number of supporting elements to create context and fill the composition naturally.
- Maintain strong visual hierarchy: one dominant concept, a few supporting cues, and a controlled amount of negative space.
- The image should be understandable at a glance, but still thoughtful enough to reward a closer look.
- Use abstract shapes, simple objects, arrows, paths, nodes, layers, frames, or symbolic elements only when they help express the central idea.
- Avoid clutter, visual noise, excessive icons, and overly complex systems.
- The final image should feel spacious but not empty.

Relationship to the text:
- The illustration should complement the text emotionally and conceptually.
- It should make the text feel more approachable, memorable, or clear.
- It should not duplicate the text.
- It should not function as a standalone infographic that tries to teach the whole topic.
- It should visually emphasize the most important idea that a reader should carry with them.
- The image should feel like an elegant visual metaphor or simplified conceptual diagram.

Words and labels:
- Words are allowed, but should be used sparingly and only when they improve comprehension.
- Do not include a heading or title.
- Avoid paragraphs, captions, legends, long labels, or dense annotations.
- If text is used, keep it to a few short labels, single words, or very brief callouts.
- Text should be secondary to the visual composition.
- The image must still work if the viewer only glances at it.

What to avoid:
- Do not create a busy infographic.
- Do not show every concept from the text.
- Do not turn the image into a flowchart unless the text’s core idea is truly a flow or sequence.
- Do not overuse arrows, labels, or icons.
- Do not create a literal scene if an abstract conceptual visual would be clearer.
- Do not add a title, heading, logo, watermark, border, or frame.
- Do not use photorealistic people, mascots, cartoon characters, or decorative filler.
- Do not use shadows, gradients, textures, or dramatic lighting.
- Do not make the image feel like a slide full of information.
- Do not include unnecessary visual elements just because they are mentioned in the text.
- Do not make the central illustration too small.
- Do not leave large unused blank bands at the top, bottom, or sides of the square canvas.

Decision-making guidance:
Before creating the image, silently determine:
1. What is the single most important idea in the text?
2. What part of that idea is best supported by a visual?
3. What can be left to the written text instead of being shown?
4. What simple metaphor, structure, or relationship would make the concept easier to grasp?
5. How can the image remain minimal while still feeling meaningful?
6. How can the composition use the square canvas fully without becoming cluttered?

Final aesthetic:
The final image should be minimal, professional, editorial, intelligent, and restrained. It should feel like a premium conceptual hero illustration: clean line art, pure white background, selective color accents, sparse text, strong use of the square canvas, and a clear visual focus on the most important idea from the provided text.
